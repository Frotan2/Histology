# Chapter 1 — Histology & Its Methods of Study

### Part A — Recall

**Q1.** In H&E staining, nuclei appear blue because:

A) Nucleic acids are negatively charged and bind the basic dye hematoxylin
B) Proteins bind hematoxylin
C) Eosin is a basic dye
D) DNA binds eosin

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** DNA/RNA phosphate groups are negatively charged and bind the basic (cationic) dye hematoxylin → blue. Eosin is an acidic (anionic) dye that binds basic proteins → pink. Protein (B) is wrong — proteins bind eosin, not hematoxylin. (C) reverses eosin's nature. (D) reverses DNA's binding partner.

**Decisive feature:** Basophilic structures (nucleic acids) bind basic dyes; acidophilic (eosinophilic) structures (proteins) bind acidic dyes.

**Learning Objective:** LO-1 (H&E mechanism, basophilia vs eosinophilia).

---

### Part B — Understanding

**Q2.** Which of the following stains REQUIRES a frozen section?

A) Oil Red O
B) Prussian blue
C) Von Kossa
D) PAS

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** Oil Red O is a lysochrome (fat-soluble dye) that dissolves in lipid droplets — but routine paraffin processing uses organic solvents (xylene, alcohol) that extract the lipid. Frozen sections preserve the lipid for staining. Prussian blue (iron), Von Kossa (calcium), and PAS (glycogen/basement membranes) all work on paraffin sections.

**Decisive feature:** Lipid dyes → frozen section; the others are aqueous or work on mineralised/proteinaceous material in paraffin.

**Learning Objective:** LO-4 (special stains and section preparation).

---

### Part C — Application

**Q3.** A pathology resident obtains a lymph node, fixes it in formalin, and processes it for paraffin embedding. The pathologist wants to demonstrate lipid-laden macrophages. The slide will show:

A) Bright red droplets in macrophages
B) Empty-appearing vacuoles where droplets were extracted
C) Brown granular pigment
D) Basophilic granular pigment

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Paraffin processing uses xylene and graded alcohols, which extract neutral lipid. The lipid is gone, leaving only the round outline of the former droplet — an empty vacuole. To *see* the lipid, the resident should have requested a frozen section and Oil Red O.

**Decisive feature:** Lipid solubility in processing solvents → empty vacuoles in paraffin sections.

**Learning Objective:** LO-4 (artefacts of routine processing; when to use frozen section).

---

### Part D — Integration

**Q4.** Immunohistochemistry for CD68 (macrophage marker) is being developed on a paraffin section. Antigen retrieval is necessary because:

A) Formalin cross-links mask epitopes
B) Macrophages are rare in paraffin sections
C) CD68 is only on frozen tissue
D) Eosin interferes with antibody binding

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** Formalin fixation creates methylene bridges between proteins, cross-linking antigens and partially masking epitopes. Heat-induced epitope retrieval (HIER) or proteolytic digestion unmasks them. CD68 is robustly detected on paraffin sections with retrieval. Eosin does not block IHC (eosin is removed in processing). The marker works on paraffin.

**Decisive feature:** Formalin cross-linking → epitope masking → need for retrieval.

**Learning Objective:** LO-3 (immunohistochemistry principles, antigen retrieval).

### Part E — High-Difficulty

**Q5.** A research lab is comparing TEM and confocal microscopy for studying mitochondria in living hepatocytes. The decisive advantage of confocal over TEM for this purpose is:

A) Higher resolution
B) Ability to image living cells with fluorescent markers
C) Better visualisation of internal mitochondrial cristae
D) Lower cost

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** TEM requires fixed, dehydrated, sectioned, heavy-metal-stained specimens — no live imaging possible. Confocal microscopy uses fluorescent dyes (e.g., MitoTracker) in living cells and optical sectioning to generate 3-D reconstructions. TEM has *higher* resolution (A, C reversed), is *more* expensive (D reversed).

**Decisive feature:** Live-cell imaging with fluorescent markers is unique to confocal (and other fluorescence) microscopy.

**Learning Objective:** LO-2 (TEM vs light vs fluorescence/confocal, resolution vs live imaging).

# Chapter 2 — The Cytoplasm

### Part A — Recall

**Q1.** Mitochondrial inner-membrane invaginations that increase surface area for the electron transport chain are called:

A) Cristae
B) Thylakoids
C) Cisternae
D) Lamellae

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** Mitochondrial cristae are the inner-membrane folds that house the ETC complexes and ATP synthase. Thylakoids are plant chloroplast membranes. Cisternae are flattened RER/Golgi stacks. Lamellae is a generic term for stacked membranes (used in chloroplast too).

**Decisive feature:** Cristae = mitochondrial ETC surface.

**Learning Objective:** LO-2 (mitochondrial structure–function).

---

### Part B — Understanding

**Q2.** Steroid-secreting cells (adrenal cortex, Leydig) have abundant smooth ER because:

A) Smooth ER detoxifies drugs
B) Smooth ER is the site of steroid hormone synthesis
C) Smooth ER stores Ca²⁺ for muscle
D) Smooth ER makes membrane proteins

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Cytochrome P450 enzymes (e.g., CYP17, CYP21, CYP11) on the SER membrane catalyse the conversion of cholesterol → pregnenolone → downstream steroids. Adrenal cortex fasciculata spongiocytes have extensive SER (the "foamy" appearance is lipid droplets + abundant SER).

**Decisive feature:** SER = lipid/steroid metabolism; steroid cells = abundant SER + lipid droplets.

**Learning Objective:** LO-5 (organelle specialisation in secretory cells).

---

### Part C — Application

**Q3.** A patient is prescribed a barbiturate that induces cytochrome P450 enzymes chronically. Hepatocytes would be expected to show:

A) Decreased SER, decreased eosinophilia
B) Increased SER, increased cytoplasmic eosinophilia
C) Increased RER, increased basophilia
D) Decreased mitochondria, decreased ATP

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** CYP450 induction requires synthesis of new SER membrane (the enzymes are integral membrane proteins). This expands the SER compartment and increases overall cytoplasmic eosinophilia (SER has no ribosomes, hence eosinophilic). The classic teaching is that phenobarbital induces SER proliferation visible as "ground-glass" eosinophilic cytoplasm on biopsy.

**Decisive feature:** CYP induction → SER proliferation → increased cytoplasmic eosinophilia.

**Learning Objective:** LO-7 (organelle adaptation to drug exposure).

---

### Part D — Integration

**Q4.** A defect in dynein would most directly impair:

A) Anterograde axonal transport
B) Retrograde axonal transport
C) Mitochondrial ATP synthesis
D) Protein synthesis on the RER

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Dynein is the motor protein for retrograde (terminal → cell body) transport along microtubules. Kinesin drives anterograde (cell body → terminal). Dynein defects cause motor and sensory neuropathy (e.g., some forms of Charcot-Marie-Tooth).

**Decisive feature:** Kinesin = anterograde; dynein = retrograde.

**Learning Objective:** LO-6 (cytoskeletal motors and intracellular transport).

### Part E — High-Difficulty

**Q5.** A missense mutation in IFT88 (intraflagellar transport protein) in a collecting duct principal cell would most likely impair:

A) Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase function
B) Aquaporin-2 trafficking to the apical membrane
C) Mitochondrial cristae formation
D) Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channel function

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Primary cilia in the collecting duct sense urinary flow; IFT is required for ciliogenesis and apical trafficking of proteins including AQP2 in response to vasopressin. Defective IFT → defective cilia → defective AQP2 trafficking → impaired urinary concentration (nephronophthisis).

**Decisive feature:** IFT = ciliary assembly + apical trafficking; defective → impaired cilia-dependent functions.

**Learning Objective:** LO-8 (organelle integration in cell physiology).

# Chapter 3 — The Cell Nucleus

### Part A — Recall

**Q1.** The nucleolus is the site of:

A) DNA replication
B) Ribosomal RNA synthesis and ribosome assembly
C) mRNA splicing
D) Nuclear pore assembly

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** The nucleolus contains nucleolar organiser regions (NORs) on acrocentric chromosomes (13, 14, 15, 21, 22) where RNA Pol I transcribes rRNA. rRNA is processed and combined with ribosomal proteins to form the 40S and 60S subunits. DNA replication occurs in the nucleoplasm during S phase. mRNA splicing occurs in nuclear speckles.

**Decisive feature:** Nucleolus = rRNA + ribosome biogenesis.

**Learning Objective:** LO-1 (nuclear sub-compartments and their functions).

---

### Part B — Understanding

**Q2.** Euchromatin is transcriptionally active because:

A) It is tightly packed
B) It is loosely packed and accessible to transcription factors
C) It binds heterochromatin protein 1
D) It contains methylated CpG islands

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Euchromatin is decondensed, accessible, and actively transcribed. Heterochromatin (tightly packed) is largely silent. HP1 binds heterochromatin (B reversed). Methylated CpG islands are associated with gene silencing (D reversed).

**Decisive feature:** Loose = active (euchromatin); dense = silent (heterochromatin).

**Learning Objective:** LO-3 (chromatin structure → transcription).

---

### Part C — Application

**Q3.** A cell has lost functional p53. The most direct consequence for cell-cycle control is:

A) Loss of G1 DNA-damage checkpoint
B) Loss of mitotic spindle checkpoint
C) Loss of cytokinesis
D) Loss of S-phase entry

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** p53 is the master regulator of the G1 DNA-damage checkpoint. DNA damage → ATM/ATR → p53 phosphorylation → p21 transcription → CDK2 inhibition → G1 arrest. Loss of p53 allows cells with damaged DNA to proceed to S phase, accumulating mutations — a hallmark of cancer (p53 is mutated in ~50% of human cancers).

**Decisive feature:** p53 = G1 checkpoint; loss → genomic instability.

**Learning Objective:** LO-7 (cell-cycle checkpoints and cancer).

### Part D — Integration

**Q4.** A biopsy shows hepatocytes with deeply eosinophilic cytoplasm containing Mallory-Denk bodies (cytokeratin aggregates). The most likely associated finding is:

A) Centriacinar emphysema
B) Alcoholic steatohepatitis
C) Cardiac failure
D) Biliary obstruction

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Mallory-Denk bodies (cytokeratin K8/K18 aggregates, ubiquitin-positive) are characteristic of alcoholic hepatitis and NASH. The eosinophilic cytoplasm reflects mitochondrial injury and SER proliferation.

**Decisive feature:** Mallory-Denk bodies = alcoholic/NASH hepatocellular injury.

**Learning Objective:** LO-9 (cellular pathology correlates).

### Part E — High-Difficulty

**Q5.** A patient has a germline LMNA mutation. The most likely clinical manifestation is:

A) Marfanoid habitus
B) Hutchinson-Gilford progeria
C) Ehlers-Danlos syndrome
D) Scurvy

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** LMNA encodes lamin A/C, a nuclear lamina protein. Mutations cause a spectrum of laminopathies including Hutchinson-Gilford progeria (accelerated ageing), Emery-Dreifuss muscular dystrophy, and dilated cardiomyopathy. Marfanoid habitus is FBN1 (fibrillin-1). Ehlers-Danlos is collagen. Scurvy is vitamin C deficiency.

**Decisive feature:** LMNA = nuclear lamina; mutations → progeria/laminopathies.

**Learning Objective:** LO-8 (nuclear envelope in disease).

# Chapter 4 — Epithelial Tissue

### Part A — Recall

**Q1.** Which intercellular junction forms a continuous belt around the apex of epithelial cells and separates apical from basolateral membrane proteins?

A) Desmosome
B) Gap junction
C) Tight junction (zonula occludens)
D) Hemidesmosome

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** Tight junctions (claudins, occludin) form paired sealing strands that (1) seal paracellular space and (2) act as a *fence* to prevent lateral diffusion of apical vs basolateral membrane proteins. Desmosomes are spot adhesions. Gap junctions are channels. Hemidesmosomes are basal (not a belt).

**Decisive feature:** ZO-1 + claudins + belt shape + fence function.

**Learning Objective:** LO-2 (junction structure–function).

---

### Part B — Understanding

**Q2.** Type II pneumocytes are critical for alveolar homeostasis because they:

A) Mediate gas exchange across the blood-air barrier
B) Secrete pulmonary surfactant and serve as alveolar stem cells
C) Phagocytose inhaled debris
D) Form the capillary basement membrane

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Type II pneumocytes produce surfactant (DPPC + SP-A/B/C/D, packaged in lamellar bodies), reducing alveolar surface tension and stabilising alveoli across breaths. They also serve as stem cells, proliferating and differentiating into type I cells after injury. Type I cells mediate gas exchange. Alveolar macrophages clear debris.

**Decisive feature:** Type II = surfactant + stem cell; Type I = gas exchange.

**Learning Objective:** LO-3 (epithelial cell specialisation).

### Part C — Application

**Q3.** A patient presents with flaccid intraepidermal blisters; biopsy shows acantholysis (rounded keratinocytes, "tombstone" basal layer); DIF shows IgG in a net-like pattern around keratinocytes. The target antigen is:

A) Hemidesmosomal BP180
B) Desmosomal desmoglein 3
C) Laminin-332
D) Type VII collagen

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Pemphigus vulgaris = autoantibody against desmoglein 3 (and sometimes desmoglein 1) → desmosome disruption → intraepidermal acantholysis → flaccid blisters. BP180 → bullous pemphigoid (subepidermal split). Laminin-332 → junctional epidermolysis bullosa. Type VII collagen → dystrophic EB.

**Decisive feature:** Intraepidermal split + acantholysis + net-like DIF = pemphigus vulgaris.

**Learning Objective:** LO-8 (skin/clinical correlate via epithelial junctions).

### Part D — Integration

**Q4.** A patient has chronic acid reflux and biopsy of the distal esophagus shows columnar epithelium with goblet cells. The diagnostic interpretation is:

A) Esophageal adenocarcinoma
B) Barrett's esophagus (intestinal metaplasia)
C) Reflux esophagitis
D) Squamous cell carcinoma

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Barrett's esophagus = metaplasia of stratified squamous non-keratinised epithelium → columnar with goblet cells (intestinal-type) in response to chronic acid injury. The goblet cells specifically define Barrett's. It is a precursor to esophageal adenocarcinoma but not cancer in itself.

**Decisive feature:** Columnar + goblet cells in distal esophagus = Barrett's (intestinal metaplasia).

**Learning Objective:** LO-9 (metaplasia as a clinical concept).

### Part E — High-Difficulty

**Q5.** A 30-week premature infant develops grunting respirations and diffuse ground-glass opacities on chest X-ray. The structural defect underlying the gas-exchange failure is:

A) Absence of type I pneumocytes
B) Surfactant deficiency with alveolar collapse
C) Pulmonary capillary maldevelopment
D) Cartilage deficiency in the trachea

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (NRDS) in premature infants is due to immature type II pneumocytes that do not yet produce adequate surfactant. Without surfactant, alveolar surface tension rises → alveolar collapse (atelectasis) → impaired gas exchange. Antenatal corticosteroids accelerate type II maturation; postnatal surfactant instillation is the treatment.

**Decisive feature:** Premature + grunting + ground-glass = surfactant deficiency.

**Learning Objective:** LO-9 (clinical correlate of epithelial function).

# Chapter 5 — Connective Tissue

### Part A — Recall

**Q1.** Type I collagen is found in greatest abundance in:

A) Basement membranes
B) Hyaline cartilage
C) Bone, tendon, dermis, sclera
D) Reticular lamina of lymphoid organs

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** Type I collagen is the most abundant collagen (~90% of body collagen); found in bone, tendon, dermis, sclera, organ capsules. Type II = hyaline/elastic cartilage. Type IV = basement membrane. Type III (reticular) = lymphoid stroma, BM reticular lamina.

**Decisive feature:** Type I = bulk structural (bone, tendon, dermis).

**Learning Objective:** LO-1 (collagen types and distribution).

---

### Part B — Understanding

**Q2.** Fibroblasts become activated myofibroblasts during wound healing primarily in response to:

A) Epidermal growth factor
B) Transforming growth factor-β
C) Vascular endothelial growth factor
D) Interleukin-2

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** TGF-β released by platelets and macrophages activates fibroblasts → expression of α-smooth muscle actin → myofibroblast → contraction of the wound (wound contraction). Excess TGF-β signalling drives fibrosis (keloid, scleroderma, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic cirrhosis).

**Decisive feature:** TGF-β = master fibrogenic cytokine.

**Learning Objective:** LO-7 (CT dynamics in wound healing).

---

### Part C — Application

**Q3.** A patient presents with hyperextensible skin, hypermobile joints, and easy bruising. A biopsy shows abnormal collagen fibril packing. A likely mutation is in:

A) COL4A3 (type IV collagen)
B) FBN1 (fibrillin-1)
C) COL5A1 (type V collagen)
D) ELN (elastin)

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** Ehlers-Danlos syndrome classical type is caused by COL5A1/COL5A2 mutations (type V collagen regulates type I fibril diameter and packing). COL4A3 → Alport syndrome. FBN1 → Marfan syndrome. ELN → supravalvular aortic stenosis (Williams syndrome) or cutis laxa.

**Decisive feature:** EDS classical = COL5A1/2 (type V); hyperextensibility + hypermobility.

**Learning Objective:** LO-9 (collagen disease correlates).

---

### Part D — Integration

**Q4.** Scurvy results from vitamin C deficiency because:

A) Vitamin C is a cofactor for lysyl oxidase
B) Vitamin C is a cofactor for prolyl/lysyl hydroxylase
C) Vitamin C directly cross-links collagen
D) Vitamin C activates fibroblasts

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Prolyl and lysyl hydroxylases in the RER require vitamin C (ascorbate) as a cofactor to hydroxylate proline and lysine residues in nascent pro-α chains. Without hydroxyproline and hydroxylysine, the triple helix is unstable, collagen cannot form properly, and connective tissues fail — manifesting as bleeding gums, poor wound healing, perifollicular haemorrhages, and bone pain. Lysyl oxidase (cross-linking) requires copper, not vitamin C.

**Decisive feature:** Vitamin C = prolyl/lysyl hydroxylase cofactor; deficiency = scurvy.

**Learning Objective:** LO-7 (collagen synthesis pathway and disease).

### Part E — High-Difficulty

**Q5.** In the *ob/ob* mouse (leptin-deficient) model of obesity, the principal histological signature of adipose tissue dysfunction is:

A) Brown adipocyte hyperplasia
B) Multilocular adipocytes replacing white
C) Crown-like structures of macrophages around hypertrophic adipocytes
D) Complete absence of adipocytes

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** Obese adipose is hypoxic (adipocyte hypertrophy outpaces angiogenesis), and hypertrophic adipocytes secrete MCP-1 → macrophage recruitment → formation of crown-like structures (macrophages ringed around a dead or dying adipocyte). M1 polarisation drives chronic inflammation (TNF-α, IL-6) and contributes to insulin resistance.

**Decisive feature:** Crown-like structures + M1 macrophages = inflamed obese adipose.

**Learning Objective:** LO-9 (CT inflammation in metabolic disease).

# Chapter 6 — Adipose Tissue

### Part A — Recall

**Q1.** A unilocular cell with a peripheral nucleus and large central lipid droplet is characteristic of:

A) Brown adipose
B) White adipose
C) Beige adipose
D) Cartilage

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** White adipocytes have a single large lipid droplet that displaces the cytoplasm and nucleus to the periphery → "signet-ring" appearance. Brown adipocytes are multilocular. Beige adipocytes are intermediate, induced in white fat by cold/β-adrenergic stimuli.

**Decisive feature:** Unilocular + peripheral nucleus = white adipocyte.

**Learning Objective:** LO-1 (adipose cell types).

---

### Part B — Understanding

**Q2.** Brown adipose tissue produces heat through:

A) ATP-dependent futile cycling
B) UCP1-mediated proton leak across the inner mitochondrial membrane
C) Increased glycolysis
D) Increased lipogenesis

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1, thermogenin) in the inner mitochondrial membrane allows protons pumped by the electron transport chain to re-enter the matrix without passing through ATP synthase, dissipating the proton gradient as heat (non-shivering thermogenesis). Cold exposure → sympathetic NE → β3 receptor → cAMP → PKA → lipolysis → UCP1 activation.

**Decisive feature:** UCP1 = proton leak = heat (uncoupled from ATP synthesis).

**Learning Objective:** LO-4 (BAT molecular mechanism).

---

### Part C — Application

**Q3.** A morbidly obese patient has fasting glucose 220, BP 150/95, and triglycerides 320. Histology of visceral fat shows enlarged adipocytes and macrophages forming ring-like structures around individual cells. The principal driver of the insulin resistance is:

A) Decreased leptin production
B) Increased UCP1 in beige fat
C) Chronic inflammation with M1 macrophage cytokine secretion
D) Increased brown adipose mass

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** Macrophage infiltration of obese adipose (crown-like structures) drives chronic low-grade inflammation; M1 macrophages secrete TNF-α and IL-6, which impair insulin signalling (serine phosphorylation of IRS-1). This is the cellular basis of obesity-induced insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome.

**Decisive feature:** Crown-like structures + M1 cytokines → insulin resistance.

**Learning Objective:** LO-7 (adipose inflammation → metabolic disease).

---

### Part D — Integration

**Q4.** Pioglitazone (a thiazolidinedione) improves insulin sensitivity in type 2 diabetes primarily because it:

A) Directly stimulates insulin release
B) Activates PPAR-γ, promoting adipocyte differentiation and visceral fat redistribution
C) Inhibits hepatic gluconeogenesis
D) Blocks intestinal glucose absorption

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Thiazolidinediones are synthetic ligands for PPAR-γ, the master regulator of adipogenesis. PPAR-γ activation promotes recruitment of new, small, insulin-sensitive adipocytes (hyperplasia rather than hypertrophy), reduces ectopic lipid deposition in muscle and liver, and shifts fat from visceral to subcutaneous depots — improving insulin sensitivity.

**Decisive feature:** PPAR-γ agonist → healthier adipose distribution → insulin sensitisation.

**Learning Objective:** LO-8 (adipogenesis and pharmacology).

### Part E — High-Difficulty

**Q5.** A patient has recurrent fasting hypoglycaemia; the adipose biopsy shows near-complete absence of white adipose tissue, severe insulin resistance, and leptin deficiency. The most likely diagnosis is:

A) Cushing syndrome
B) Familial partial lipodystrophy (LMNA mutation)
C) Anorexia nervosa
D) Obesity-induced leptin resistance

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Familial partial lipodystrophy (Dunnigan type) results from LMNA mutations; loss of subcutaneous fat → ectopic lipid deposition in liver and muscle → severe insulin resistance, hypertriglyceridaemia, and leptin deficiency. Cushing syndrome has central obesity. Anorexia nervosa has low leptin but no insulin resistance. Obesity has high leptin with leptin resistance.

**Decisive feature:** LMNA mutation + selective fat loss + ectopic lipid = lipodystrophy.

**Learning Objective:** LO-9 (clinical correlate of adipose pathology).

# Chapter 7 — Nerve Tissue

### Part A — Recall

**Q1.** Which glial cell forms myelin in the peripheral nervous system?

A) Oligodendrocyte
B) Astrocyte
C) Schwann cell
D) Microglia

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** Schwann cells form myelin around peripheral axons (one internode per cell). Oligodendrocytes form myelin in the CNS (multiple internodes per cell). Astrocytes support and contribute to the BBB. Microglia are CNS macrophages.

**Decisive feature:** PNS myelin = Schwann cell; CNS myelin = oligodendrocyte.

**Learning Objective:** LO-1 (glial cell types and functions).

---

### Part B — Understanding

**Q2.** The myelin sheath increases nerve conduction velocity primarily by:

A) Increasing the density of voltage-gated Na⁺ channels
B) Reducing membrane capacitance and increasing membrane resistance
C) Generating additional ATP
D) Increasing the axon diameter alone

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Myelin reduces membrane capacitance (charge stored across the bilayer is reduced by the multilamellar insulation) and increases membrane resistance (less current leak). Combined with concentration of Na⁺ channels at nodes of Ranvier, this enables *saltatory* conduction — action potentials "jump" from node to node at velocities up to ~120 m/s.

**Decisive feature:** Capacitance ↓ + resistance ↑ = faster saltatory conduction.

**Learning Objective:** LO-4 (myelination mechanism).

---

### Part C — Application

**Q3.** A 35-year-old presents with episodes of unilateral vision loss, limb weakness, and ataxia that resolve over days; MRI shows periventricular white-matter plaques. The pathological process is:

A) PNS demyelination by autoantibodies
B) CNS demyelination with relative axonal preservation in early disease
C) Primary axonal degeneration
D) Neuronal loss without demyelination

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Multiple sclerosis = autoimmune demyelination of CNS white matter (oligodendrocyte loss). Early in the disease, axons are relatively preserved; remyelination by OPCs can occur but is incomplete and declines with age. Plaques show loss of myelin, relative axonal preservation, gliosis, and inflammatory infiltrate. PNS demyelination = Guillain-Barré.

**Decisive feature:** Periventricular plaques + relapsing-remitting course = MS (CNS demyelination).

**Learning Objective:** LO-9 (demyelinating disease correlate).

### Part D — Integration

**Q4.** A defect in the gene for connexin-26 (GJB2) is the most common genetic cause of sensorineural deafness because:

A) Connexin-26 forms gap junctions essential for K⁺ recycling in the cochlea
B) Connexin-26 is the structural protein of stereocilia
C) Connexin-26 is the neurotransmitter at hair cell synapses
D) Connexin-26 forms tight junctions in the organ of Corti

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** Connexin-26 forms gap junctions that mediate K⁺ recycling between supporting cells in the cochlea; this recycling is essential for maintaining the endocochlear potential and hair-cell function. Loss-of-function mutations → autosomal recessive nonsyndromic deafness (DFNB1). Stereocilia are actin. Neurotransmitter at hair cells is glutamate.

**Decisive feature:** Cx26 gap junctions = cochlear K⁺ recycling → deafness if mutated.

**Learning Objective:** LO-8 (gap junctions in organ physiology).

**Q5.** A newborn fails to pass meconium; rectal biopsy shows absent ganglion cells in submucosal and myenteric plexuses. The embryological defect is:

A) Failure of neural tube closure
B) Failure of neural crest cell migration to the distal colon
C) Failure of mesoderm formation

# Chapter 8 — Circulatory System

### Part A — Recall

**Q1.** Which vessel layer is thickest in medium-sized muscular arteries and is the principal effector of vascular tone?

A) Tunica intima
B) Tunica media
C) Tunica adventitia
D) Vasa vasorum

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** The tunica media of muscular arteries contains concentric layers of smooth muscle cells between internal and external elastic laminae. Smooth muscle contracts in response to α1-adrenergic stimulation (vasoconstriction) and relaxes with β2 stimulation (vasodilation). The intima is the endothelial lining; adventitia is the outer CT.

**Decisive feature:** Smooth muscle in media = tone regulation.

**Learning Objective:** LO-2 (artery wall organisation).

---

### Part B — Understanding

**Q2.** Capillaries are classified into continuous, fenestrated, and sinusoidal based on:

A) The size of the lumen
B) The structure of the endothelial cell and its basement membrane
C) The number of red blood cells they carry
D) The type of blood they contain

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Capillary types differ in endothelial structure:
- *Continuous* — tight junctions, no fenestrae; muscle, CNS (BBB), lung.
- *Fenestrated* — ~60–80 nm pores with diaphragms; endocrine glands, intestinal villi, choroid plexus.
- *Sinusoidal* — large irregular lumen, large fenestrae without diaphragms, discontinuous BM; liver, spleen, bone marrow.

**Decisive feature:** Endothelial structure defines capillary type and tissue function.

**Learning Objective:** LO-4 (capillary heterogeneity).

---

### Part C — Application

**Q3.** A patient has chronic liver cirrhosis and presents with upper GI bleeding. Endoscopy shows dilated submucosal veins in the lower esophagus. The pathogenesis is:

A) Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma
B) Portal hypertension with collateral venous flow through esophageal submucosal veins
C) Barrett's esophagus with vascular ectasia
D) Mallory-Weiss tear

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Cirrhosis → portal hypertension → portosystemic shunting through collateral veins (left gastric → esophageal venous plexus → azygos). Esophageal varices are dilated submucosal veins at risk of rupture with massive upper GI bleeding. A Mallory-Weiss tear is a longitudinal mucosal tear from retching.

**Decisive feature:** Cirrhosis + esophageal varices = portal hypertension shunting.

**Learning Objective:** LO-9 (vascular pathology correlate).

### Part D — Integration

# Chapter 16 — Endocrine System

### Part A — Recall

**Q1.** Which cell type of the anterior pituitary produces ACTH?

A) Somatotroph
B) Lactotroph
C) Corticotroph (basophil)
D) Gonadotroph

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** Corticotrophs are PAS-positive basophils that synthesise POMC (proopiomelanocortin), which is cleaved into ACTH, MSH, β-lipotropin, and other peptides. ACTH stimulates the adrenal cortex (mainly zona fasciculata) to produce cortisol. The B-FLAT mnemonic: B = FSH/LH, F = FSH/LH, L = LH, A = ACTH, T = TSH (basophils in this order).

**Decisive feature:** ACTH = corticotroph (basophil) = PAS+.

**Learning Objective:** LO-2 (anterior pituitary cytology).

### Part B — Understanding

**Q2.** The adrenal cortex zona glomerulosa produces aldosterone primarily in response to:

A) ACTH
B) Renin-angiotensin and serum potassium
C) Sympathetic stimulation
D) Cortisol feedback

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** The zona glomerulosa expresses CYP11B2 (aldosterone synthase), which catalyses the final steps of aldosterone synthesis. Regulation is independent of ACTH (which regulates zona fasciculata cortisol); aldosterone is controlled by the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) and serum K⁺. Low renal perfusion → renin → angiotensin II → aldosterone → Na⁺ reabsorption, K⁺ excretion, water retention.

**Decisive feature:** Aldosterone regulation = RAAS + K⁺ (not ACTH).

**Learning Objective:** LO-4 (adrenal cortex physiology).

### Part C — Application

**Q3.** A 35-year-old with episodic hypertension, headaches, and palpitations has elevated plasma metanephrines; imaging shows an adrenal mass. Histology of the mass shows nests of cells with a Zellballen pattern. The diagnosis is:

A) Adrenal cortical adenoma
B) Pheochromocytoma
C) Ganglioneuroma
D) Adrenal hyperplasia

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Pheochromocytoma = catecholamine-secreting tumour of the adrenal medulla chromaffin cells. Classic clinical triad: episodic hypertension, headaches, palpitations ("Pheo" mnemonic). Histology: nests of polygonal cells (Zellballen pattern) with eosinophilic granular cytoplasm, often with hyaline globules; chromaffin reaction positive; S100+ sustentacular cells surround nests; IHC positive for chromogranin and synaptophysin.

**Decisive feature:** Episodic HTN + elevated metanephrines + Zellballen = pheochromocytoma.

**Learning Objective:** LO-9 (adrenal medulla pathology correlate).

### Part D — Integration

**Q4.** A 50-year-old woman has fatigue, weight gain, and a goiter; thyroid biopsy shows lymphocytic infiltrate with germinal centres and Hürthle cell change. The diagnosis is:

A) Graves disease
B) Hashimoto thyroiditis
C) Subacute granulomatous (de Quervain) thyroiditis
D) Riedel thyroiditis

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Hashimoto thyroiditis = chronic autoimmune thyroiditis with anti-TPO and anti-thyroglobulin antibodies. Histology: dense lymphocytic infiltrate with germinal centres (replacing thyroid follicles), Hürthle (Askanazy) cells = oxyphilic metaplasia of follicular cells with abundant mitochondria (deeply eosinophilic cytoplasm), and variable fibrosis. Patients are typically hypothyroid (some pass through a transient thyrotoxic phase). Graves is hyperthyroid with scalloped colloid. De Quervain has granulomas. Riedel has dense fibrosis.

**Decisive feature:** Lymphocytic infiltrate + germinal centres + Hürthle cells = Hashimoto.

**Learning Objective:** LO-8 (thyroid pathology correlate).

### Part E — High-Difficulty

**Q5.** A newborn has ambiguous genitalia, hypotension, and hyperkalaemia; 17-hydroxyprogesterone is markedly elevated. The enzyme deficiency is:

A) 21-hydroxylase (CYP21)
B) 11β-hydroxylase (CYP11B1)
C) 17α-hydroxylase (CYP17)
D) 5α-reductase

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** 21-hydroxylase deficiency (CYP21A2) is the most common cause of congenital adrenal hyperplasia (~95% of cases). Impaired cortisol and aldosterone synthesis → loss of negative feedback → ACTH-driven adrenal hyperplasia → shunting of steroid precursors into the androgen pathway → virilisation of female fetuses (ambiguous genitalia at birth). Salt-wasting form (~75%) has aldosterone deficiency → hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia, hypotension, and shock. 17-hydroxyprogesterone accumulates and is diagnostic.

**Decisive feature:** Ambiguous genitalia + salt wasting + ↑ 17-OHP = 21-hydroxylase deficiency CAH.

**Learning Objective:** LO-9 (steroid biosynthesis pathology correlate).
