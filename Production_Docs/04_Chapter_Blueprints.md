# Chapter Blueprints — Final Architecture for Every Chapter

## Blueprint Template (applies to all chapters, adapt when subject logically requires different order)

### 1. Opening Question
- One compelling, academically meaningful question that defines what chapter will teach.
- Not dramatic, but curiosity-driving.
- Example: "How does a pathologist turn a lump of tissue into a diagnosis you can see under a microscope — and why does the choice of stain decide what you will find?" (Chapter 1 — keep, good)

### 2. Why This Matters
- Short paragraph (3-5 sentences) explaining why topic matters in medicine and/or exam.
- No motivational prose.
- Include 1-sentence clinical case hook.

### 3. Learning Objectives
- 5-10 precise, measurable objectives using verbs: explain, distinguish, classify, predict, identify, compare, relate, interpret, apply.
- Each objective must be supported by teaching content → mastery check → question bank coverage.

### 4. Big Picture
- Mental map before details. Learner knows "Where are we going?"
- Use simple hierarchy, not ASCII art noise.
- 1 paragraph + 3-5 bullet map.

### 5. Core Concept
- Central principle governing chapter. One sentence that reduces chapter to single framework.
- Examples:
  - Epithelium: organization around protection, exchange, secretion, absorption, polarity.
  - Connective: cells embedded in ECM whose composition determines mechanical and biological behavior.
  - GI tract: common layered plan modified regionally for specialized functions.

### 6. Build the Concept
- Teach from basic components toward complete structure.
- Never introduce advanced terminology before foundation.
- Use "Make it feel easy" sequence: Name it → Place it → Build it → Explain why → Give its function → Connect structure to function → Distinguish it → Apply it.

### 7. Structure
- Describe cells, ECM, layers, organization, specialized structures, relevant molecular components.
- Only details contributing to understanding or exam readiness.

### 8. Function
- What structure accomplishes.

### 9. Structure → Function
- Explicitly explain why structure works for function. Do not leave for student to infer when can be clearly explained.

### 10. Classification
- Logical system, not list without principle.
- Explain principle behind classification.

### 11. Compare & Distinguish
- For commonly confused entities, concise comparisons.
- Purpose: "How do I know which one I am looking at?"
- Use table format: Feature | A | B | Key identifier

### 12. Recognition Logic
- Textual image-ready framework without requiring images.
- Format:
  - Look for...
  - Then confirm with...
  - Do not confuse with...
  - The decisive feature is...

### 13. Clinical Correlation
- Clinical material must serve histology. Answer: What histological concept explains this clinical condition?
- Keep to 2-3 correlations max, each 1 paragraph mechanism + 1 paragraph histology.

### 14. Common Misconceptions
- Keep existing strength, expand systematically.
- Format: Misconception → Why it seems plausible → Correct understanding.

### 15. High-Yield Knowledge
- Selective, not everything marked high yield.
- Must Know: facts retained exactly
- Must Distinguish: frequently confused concepts
- Must Understand: concepts not to memorize without understanding

### 16. Integrated Summary
- Concise conceptual synthesis, not repeat sentences.
- Answers: What is system? What are defining components? What makes each different? What principle connects them?

### 17. Mastery Check
- Small number open-ended prompts (5-7).
- Examples: Explain central principle, reconstruct classification, explain structure-function relationship, name decisive distinguishing features, explain one clinical correlation.

### 18. Transition
- Short conceptual bridge to next chapter. Preserve existing chapter-to-chapter narrative value.

### 19. Rapid Review
- Very concise, usable before exam.
- Only: essential classifications, decisive distinctions, critical associations, core mechanisms, major clinical correlations.

## Chapter-Specific Blueprints

### Chapter 1 — Histology & Its Methods of Study
- Opening Q: How does a pathologist turn a lump of tissue into a diagnosis you can see — and why does stain choice decide what you will find? (KEEP)
- Why Matters: Biopsy interpretation depends on processing, stain chemistry, microscopy; wrong technique = missed diagnosis.
- Core Concept: Tissue is transparent and fragile; histology = making it hard enough to cut + creating contrast; each technique reveals only property it was designed to detect.
- Build: Living tissue → autolysis → fixation (cross-linking) → dehydration (water→alcohol) → clearing (alcohol→xylene) → embedding (paraffin) → sectioning (~5 µm) → staining (charge, oxidation, metal binding, solubility) → microscopy (resolution vs contrast)
- Structure: Processing pipeline steps, fixatives (formalin LM, glutaraldehyde+osmium EM), dyes (basic vs acidic), special stains molecular targets, microscopy modalities
- Function: Each step solves one problem (preservation, hardness, contrast, resolution)
- Structure→Function: Why formalin preserves LM but not EM membranes (osmium needed for lipid fixation + electron scattering)
- Classification: Stains by mechanism (charge, oxidation→Schiff, metal reduction, lipophilic, birefringence), microscopes by contrast mechanism
- Compare: Frozen vs paraffin, TEM vs SEM vs light, H&E vs special stains
- Recognition: Blue nucleus = nucleic acid = basophilic = basic dye; pink = protein = acidophilic = acidic dye; empty vacuole = dissolved lipid; magenta BM = PAS
- Clinical: Fatty liver needs frozen/Oil Red O; amyloidosis needs Congo red + polarized; iron overload needs Prussian blue; mineralization needs Von Kossa (phosphate/carbonate indirect)
- Misconceptions: "Clearing removes water", "Von Kossa stains calcium directly", "H&E shows everything", "More magnification = more resolution"
- High-Yield: H&E chemistry, PAS targets, reticular silver, lipid frozen, resolution ~0.2 µm LM, EM fixatives, frozen vs paraffin
- Transition: You now know how tissue is prepared and stained. Next, step inside the cell itself — the machinery every stain is lighting up.

### Chapter 2 — Cytoplasm & Cytoskeleton
- Q: How does a cell build, move, and ship thousands of proteins without mixing them up?
- Core: Cytoplasm = cytosol + organelles + cytoskeleton; membrane flow (RER→Golgi→vesicles) and cytoskeleton (actin 6-8 nm, intermediate 10 nm, microtubule 25 nm) organize space and transport.
- Build: Membrane → RER (ribosome-studded, protein synthesis) → SER (lipid synthesis, detox, Ca2+ storage) → Golgi (cis→trans glycosylation, sorting) → lysosome (acid hydrolases, M6P targeting) → peroxisome (oxidase/catalase, not lysosome) → mitochondria (cristae, matrix) → cytoskeleton
- Compare: RER vs SER, lysosome vs peroxisome, microvilli (actin core) vs cilia (9+2 microtubule) vs intermediate filaments (keratin, vimentin, desmin, neurofilament, GFAP)
- Recognition: Basophilic cytoplasm = RER-rich = protein synthesis (plasma cell, pancreatic acinar); eosinophilic = mitochondria/protein-rich
- Clinical: Kartagener (dynein arm defect → immotile cilia), I-cell disease (M6P defect → lysosomal enzymes misrouted)
- Transition: Cytoplasm builds. Nucleus controls.

### Chapter 3 — Nucleus
- Q: How does 2 meters of DNA fit in a 6 µm nucleus and still remain readable?
- Core: Nucleus = envelope (double membrane + pores) + chromatin (DNA+histone) + nucleolus (rRNA) — organization around accessibility: euchromatin (active, light) vs heterochromatin (inactive, dark).
- Build: Envelope → pores (120 MDa, selective transport) → chromatin → chromosomes → nucleolus (FCC/DSC/GC, rRNA transcription)
- Recognition: Heterochromatin dark peripheral, euchromatin light central, nucleolus prominent in protein-synthesizing cells
- Clinical: Barr body (inactive X), nuclear atypia in cancer (irregular envelope, prominent nucleoli)
- Transition: Cells → tissues. How do cells organize into sheets that seal, absorb, secrete?

### Chapter 4 — Epithelium
- Q: Why does acid reflux slowly turn food pipe lining into intestine — and why does that raise cancer risk? (KEEP)
- Core: Epithelial cell is polarized (apical, lateral, basal each solves different problem) and entire epithelium organized so nothing crosses except through cells.
- Build: Defining features (polarity, avascular, basement membrane, regeneration) → classification (layers + shape + special types) → junctions (apical→basal order logical) → basement membrane (type IV collagen network + laminin + perlecan) → apical specializations (microvilli actin, cilia 9+2, stereocilia, primary cilium) → glands (merocrine/apocrine/holocrine, serous/mucous, myoepithelial)
- Compare: Simple squamous vs cuboidal vs columnar, stratified squamous keratinized vs non-keratinized, pseudostratified vs stratified, transitional (umbrella cells)
- Recognition: Sheet + BM + no vessels = epithelium; pseudostratified = all cells touch BM but nuclei at different heights; transitional = umbrella dome cells
- Clinical: Barrett metaplasia (stem cell reprogramming under chronic stress), blister level (pemphigus desmoglein intraepidermal vs pemphigoid hemidesmosome subepidermal)
- Transition: Epithelium is boundary. Connective tissue is framework.

### Chapter 5 — Connective Tissue (including integrated Cartilage & Bone)
- Q: Why do some people have stretchy skin and others fragile bones — and how can single tissue explain both?
- Core: Connective tissue = cells + ECM; matrix (fibers + ground substance) is load-bearing, space-filling, signal-carrying; its molecular composition determines every disease.
- Build: Three components → fibers (collagen I-IV master table, elastic elastin+fibrillin, reticular type III) → ground substance (GAGs hold water, proteoglycans, glycoproteins) → cells (fibroblast builder, macrophage defense, mast cell heparin/histamine metachromatic, plasma cell clock-face basophilic, adipocyte) → types (loose, dense regular/irregular, elastic, reticular, mucoid) → specializations: cartilage (hyaline type II, elastic type II + elastic fibers, fibrocartilage type I + II, perichondrium, avascular) → bone (woven vs lamellar, osteon, osteoblast/osteocyte/osteoclast, remodeling, endochondral vs intramembranous)
- Classification: By fiber ratio and arrangement; collagen types by location/disease
- Compare: Hyaline vs elastic vs fibrocartilage, dense regular vs irregular, collagen vs elastic disease (Ehlers-Danlos/OI/scurvy vs Marfan)
- Clinical: Marfan (fibrillin-1 scaffold defect → aortic aneurysm), scurvy (vit C → hydroxylation defect → weak collagen), OI (type I collagen mutation)
- Transition: Connective tissue stores energy in specialized form — adipose.

### Chapter 6 — Adipose Tissue
- Q: How does fat do more than store energy — and why does its location decide metabolic risk?
- Core: Adipose is specialized connective tissue where lipid storage dominates, but endocrine signaling (leptin, adiponectin) and thermogenesis (UCP1) make it metabolic organ.
- Build: White unilocular (signet-ring, peripheral nucleus, single droplet, leptin) vs brown multilocular (central nucleus, many mitochondria, UCP1) vs beige inducible
- Compare: White vs brown vs beige
- Clinical: Obesity, brown fat thermogenesis, lipoma vs liposarcoma
- Transition: Storage → contraction → communication. Next, tissues that move and conduct.

### Chapter 7 — Muscle Tissue (NEW, integrated from Junqueira Ch10)
- Q: How do three tissues that all contract end up looking so different — and why does that matter for heart failure and gut motility?
- Core: Muscle = actin-myosin contractile apparatus, but arrangement (sarcomere vs dense bodies), innervation (voluntary vs involuntary), and junctions (intercalated discs) determine function.
- Build: Skeletal (striated, multinucleated peripheral, T-tubule at A-I junction, triad, sarcomere Z-M, satellite cells) → Cardiac (striated, branching, single central nucleus, intercalated disc: fascia adherens + desmosome + gap junction, T-tubule at Z disc, diad) → Smooth (non-striated, single central nucleus, dense bodies, caveolae, gap junctions, slow sustained contraction)
- Compare: Skeletal vs cardiac vs smooth table: striation, nuclei, intercalated disc, T-tubule, regeneration, innervation
- Recognition: Cross-striation + peripheral nuclei = skeletal; branching + central nucleus + intercalated disc = cardiac; spindle + central nucleus + no striation = smooth
- Clinical: Myocardial infarction (loss of intercalated disc continuity), myasthenia gravis (NMJ), smooth muscle hyperplasia in asthma
- Transition: Muscle moves. Nerve controls.

### Chapter 8 — Nerve Tissue
- Q: How does a cell that lasts a lifetime wire a body and still support itself?
- Core: Neuron = long-lived, polarized, excitable cell (dendrite→soma→axon→synapse); glia = support, myelination, barrier, immune.
- Build: Neuron parts (perikaryon Nissl/RER, axon hillock, axon, terminals) → cytoskeleton (neurofilament, microtubule transport) → synapse (chemical vs electrical, active zone) → myelin PNS (one Schwann per internode, mesaxon, neurilemma) vs CNS (oligodendrocyte many internodes, no neurilemma, no basal lamina) → unmyelinated (Schwann envelops many axons) → glia (astrocyte foot processes BBB, oligodendrocyte myelin, microglia phagocytosis, ependyma)
- Compare: PNS vs CNS myelin, astrocyte vs oligodendrocyte, axon vs dendrite
- Clinical: Multiple sclerosis (CNS myelin loss), Guillain-Barré (PNS), Wallerian degeneration
- Transition: Nerve needs supply. Circulatory system connects all tissues.

### Chapter 9 — Circulatory System
- Q: How does one tube plan build an aorta that stretches and a capillary that leaks on purpose?
- Core: Vessel = intima (endothelium + subendothelial CT) + media (smooth muscle + elastic) + adventitia (CT); composition ratio determines mechanical behavior.
- Build: Heart (endocardium, myocardium, epicardium, Purkinje) → elastic artery (many elastic lamellae, recoil) → muscular artery (many smooth muscle, vasoconstriction) → arteriole (1-2 muscle layers, resistance) → capillary types (continuous tight junctions BBB, fenestrated with diaphragms endocrine/kidney, discontinuous sinusoid liver/spleen/bone marrow) → vein (large lumen, thin media, valves) → lymphatic (no BM, overlapping endothelium, anchoring filaments)
- Compare: Artery vs vein (lumen, media, elastic, valves), elastic vs muscular artery, capillary types, blood vs lymphatic capillary
- Clinical: Atherosclerosis (intima disease), varicose veins (valve failure), lymphedema
- Transition: Circulatory carries specialized connective tissue — blood.

### Chapter 10 — Blood
- Q: How can you tell a bacterial infection from a parasitic one from a single blood smear?
- Core: Blood = plasma + formed elements; leukocyte morphology (nucleus shape + granule color) predicts function.
- Build: RBC (biconcave disc ~7.5 µm, no nucleus, no organelles, eosinophilic) → neutrophil (~12-15 µm, 3-5 lobes, neutrophilic granules, bacterial defense) → eosinophil (bilobed, large eosinophilic granules, parasite/allergy) → basophil (bilobed obscured, basophilic metachromatic granules, heparin/histamine) → small lymphocyte (~6-8 µm, round dark nucleus, scant cytoplasm, T vs B not distinguishable by morphology) → large lymphocyte/activated → monocyte (~12-20 µm, kidney-shaped nucleus, precursor to macrophage) → platelet (discoid, no nucleus, granules)
- Recognition: Look for nucleus shape → then granule → then size
- Clinical: Left shift (band neutrophils), eosinophilia (parasite), lymphocytosis (viral), monocytosis (chronic infection)
- Transition: Blood cells die in days to months. Where do they come from?

### Chapter 11 — Hemopoiesis
- Q: How does marrow make 200 billion red cells a day without making a tumor?
- Core: Hemopoiesis = hierarchical differentiation in specialized niche (sinusoids, stromal cells, growth factors), with morphology reflecting synthesis activity.
- Build: Marrow organization (red vs yellow, sinusoids) → stem cell → myeloid vs lymphoid progenitors → erythropoiesis (proerythroblast basophilic RER→polychromatic→orthochromatic→reticulocyte→RBC, loss of nucleus/organelles) → granulopoiesis (myeloblast→promyelocyte azurophilic granules→myelocyte specific granules→metamyelocyte→band→segmented) → megakaryopoiesis (endomitosis, platelet demarcation) → growth factors (EPO, G-CSF, GM-CSF)
- Clinical: Aplastic anemia (niche failure), leukemia (maturation arrest)
- Transition: New lymphocytes need education before defense.

### Chapter 12 — Immune System & Lymphoid Organs
- Q: Why does losing one gland in newborn chest leave them defenseless against viruses for life?
- Core: Education vs execution: primary organs (marrow B, thymus T) produce naive lymphocytes; secondary organs (node, spleen, MALT) are where lymphocytes meet antigen; T vs B geography is diagnostic.
- Build: Primary: bone marrow B maturation, thymus cortex (immature T, blood-thymus barrier, no germinal centers) → medulla (Hassall corpuscles, negative selection) → Secondary: lymph node (cortex B follicles germinal centers, paracortex T + HEV, medulla cords/sinuses, lymph flow afferent→subcapsular→cortical→medullary→efferent) → spleen (white pulp PALS T + follicles B around central artery, red pulp cords + sinusoids, no afferent lymphatics, filters blood) → MALT (Peyer patches, tonsil crypts, M cells sample antigen)
- Compare: Thymus vs lymph node (no germinal centers vs germinal centers), spleen vs node (filters blood vs lymph, no afferent lymphatics vs afferent), B vs T zones
- Clinical: DiGeorge (no thymus → T deficiency + hypocalcemia parathyroid), follicular hyperplasia (B) vs paracortical hyperplasia (T), splenectomy → Howell-Jolly bodies, encapsulated bacteria risk
- Transition: Defense protects gut. Gut is largest interface with outside world.

### Chapter 13 — Digestive Tract
- Q: How does one tube digest a steak, absorb fat, and protect itself from its own acid?
- Core: GI tract = common 4-layer plan (mucosa: epithelium + lamina propria + muscularis mucosae, submucosa: dense irregular CT + Meissner plexus, muscularis externa: inner circular + outer longitudinal + Auerbach plexus between, serosa/adventitia) modified regionally for specialized function.
- Build: Esophagus (stratified squamous non-keratinized, submucosal glands) → stomach (rugae, pits, glands: cardia mucous, fundus/body parietal HCl+intrinsic factor + chief pepsinogen + mucous neck + enteroendocrine, pylorus mucous + G cells gastrin) → small intestine (plicae + villi + crypts, duodenum Brunner glands alkaline mucus, jejunum tall villi, ileum Peyer patches, Paneth lysozyme, enteroendocrine) → large intestine (no villi, crypts, goblet cells, taenia coli, appendix) → anal canal (transition)
- Compare: Duodenum vs jejunum vs ileum, esophagus vs stomach vs intestine, villus vs crypt
- Clinical: Barrett (squamous→columnar metaplasia), peptic ulcer (parietal HCl), celiac (villus atrophy)
- Transition: Tube needs accessory organs to process nutrients.

### Chapter 14 — Organs Associated with Digestive Tract
- Q: How does liver do 500 jobs with only a few cell types — and why does its blood flow make it vulnerable?
- Core: Liver = epithelial cords + sinusoids; three models (classic lobule central vein, portal lobule bile drainage, acinus metabolic zones) explain different functions; pancreas = exocrine serous acinar + endocrine islet.
- Build: Liver (hepatocyte plates, sinusoids fenestrated discontinuous, Kupffer macrophage, Ito stellate vitamin A, bile canaliculi, portal triad) → gallbladder (mucosa + muscle) → pancreas (serous acinar basophilic basal + eosinophilic apical zymogen, centroacinar cells, islet alpha glucagon peripheral + beta insulin central + delta somatostatin) → salivary (serous vs mucous vs mixed, myoepithelial, intercalated→striated→excretory ducts)
- Compare: Liver lobule vs acinus, serous vs mucous acinus, alpha vs beta islet position
- Clinical: Cirrhosis (fibrosis disrupts sinusoid), diabetes (beta loss), Sjögren (salivary)
- Transition: Gut absorbs air? No, lung exchanges gas with similar barrier logic.

### Chapter 15 — Respiratory System
- Q: How does an organ that must stay open also clean itself — and how does a 0.5 µm barrier let oxygen through but keep blood in?
- Core: Conducting portion (air conditioning, mucociliary clearance) → respiratory portion (gas exchange, blood-air barrier 3 layers: attenuated type I pneumocyte + fused basal laminae + endothelial cell)
- Build: Nasal cavity (respiratory pseudostratified ciliated + goblet, olfactory) → nasopharynx → larynx → trachea (C-shaped hyaline cartilage + pseudostratified ciliated + goblet + seromucous glands) → bronchus (cartilage plates, glands, pseudostratified) → bronchiole (no cartilage, no glands, Clara/club cells, simple columnar→cuboidal, smooth muscle) → terminal bronchiole → respiratory bronchiole (alveoli outpocket) → alveolar duct → sac → alveolus (type I flat gas exchange ~95% surface, type II cuboidal surfactant + progenitor, dust cell macrophage) → blood-air barrier
- Compare: Bronchus vs bronchiole (cartilage, glands, epithelium, Clara cells, goblet), type I vs II pneumocyte
- Clinical: Asthma (smooth muscle hyperplasia, goblet hyperplasia), emphysema (alveolar wall loss), hyaline membrane disease (surfactant deficiency type II)
- Transition: Lung exchanges. Skin protects.

### Chapter 16 — Skin (Integument)
- Q: How does skin be waterproof, touch-sensitive, and able to regrow after a cut?
- Core: Skin = keratinized stratified squamous epithelium (epidermis) + CT (dermis papillary loose + reticular dense irregular) + appendages; keratinization = terminal differentiation.
- Build: Epidermis layers basale (stem, hemidesmosome, melanocyte) → spinosum (desmosomes spines, Langerhans dendritic) → granulosum (keratohyalin granules) → lucidum (thick skin) → corneum (anucleate keratin) → melanocyte (melanosome, supranuclear cap) → Langerhans (antigen presentation) → Merkel (touch) → dermis papillary (Meissner corpuscle) → reticular (Pacinian) → appendages (hair follicle, arrector pili smooth muscle, sebaceous holocrine, eccrine merocrine sweat, apocrine)
- Compare: Thick vs thin skin, papillary vs reticular dermis, eccrine vs apocrine
- Clinical: Melanoma (melanocyte), basal cell carcinoma (basale), burns (stem cell survival)
- Transition: Skin is barrier. Endocrine is long-distance signaling via blood.

### Chapter 17 — Urinary System (NEW, high-yield)
- Q: How does kidney filter 180 liters a day and keep protein in blood?
- Core: Filtration barrier = 3 layers (fenestrated endothelium + GBM type IV collagen/laminin/perlecan + podocyte foot processes slit diaphragm nephrin); tubule specialization reabsorbs.
- Build: Kidney cortex/medulla → nephron: glomerulus (Bowman capsule parietal simple squamous + visceral podocytes, mesangium) → filtration barrier → proximal convoluted (brush border, eosinophilic, reabsorption) → loop of Henle thin→thick → distal (macula densa) → collecting duct (principal + intercalated) → juxtaglomerular apparatus (macula densa + juxtaglomerular cells renin + mesangial) → ureter (transitional epithelium + muscle) → bladder (umbrella cells)
- Compare: Proximal vs distal tubule, filtration barrier layers
- Clinical: Goodpasture (anti-GBM type IV collagen), Alport (type IV collagen mutation), minimal change disease (podocyte foot process effacement → proteinuria, needs TEM)
- Transition: Kidney regulates. Endocrine commands.

### Chapter 18 — Endocrine Glands
- Q: How does pea-sized gland behind eyes control growth, milk, stress, thirst — with only handful of cell types?
- Core: Endocrine = ductless, secretion into blood; identified by signature cell/hormone pairing; pituitary divides system: anterior glandular synthesizes, posterior neural stores.
- Build: Pituitary (anterior adenohypophysis oral ectoderm: acidophils GH/prolactin somatotroph/lactotroph + basophils B-FLAT: FSH/LH gonadotroph, ACTH corticotroph, TSH thyrotroph, chromophobes degranulated) + posterior neurohypophysis neural ectoderm (Herring bodies ADH/oxytocin stored, pituicytes) → hypothalamic portal system → thyroid (colloid follicles thyroglobulin, follicular cells T3/T4, parafollicular C cells calcitonin) → parathyroid (chief PTH + oxyphil) → adrenal cortex (glomerulosa aldosterone salt, fasciculata cortisol sugar clear cells SER + tubular mitochondria, reticularis androgen sex) + medulla (chromaffin catecholamines, modified postganglionic sympathetic) → pineal (pinealocyte melatonin + corpora arenacea brain sand)
- Compare: Anterior vs posterior pituitary, cortex vs medulla adrenal, thyroid vs parathyroid (colloid vs no colloid, clear chief cells)
- Clinical: Prolactinoma (acidophil lactotroph, bitemporal hemianopia optic chiasm), Graves (scalloped colloid), Cushing (fasciculata cortisol), pheochromocytoma (medulla), diabetes insipidus (ADH storage failure)
- Transition: Endocrine is command. Final synthesis: entire body is epithelium + connective tissue + muscle + nerve organized by function.
